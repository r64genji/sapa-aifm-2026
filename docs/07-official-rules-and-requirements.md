## 3. Eligibility and registration

- **Who:** "every student enrolled at a Malaysian university at diploma, foundation or undergraduate level, from any discipline." [SITE]
- No coding experience needed. Non-technical students (business, marketing, design, finance, communications) are welcome. [SITE]
- **Postgraduate students, international students, and non-university colleges:** not mentioned either way. One page says "every college & university in Malaysia". Ask the organisers if this applies to you.
- **Laptop:** required. [SITE]
- **Team size:** **1 to 5.** Solo is allowed from start to finish. The organisers do not match solo people into teams. Teams can mix universities. [SITE] [PDF has no team size.]
- **Fee:** **RM50 per participant.** The FAQ calls it a "commitment fee" for participation through to the final pitching stage. Workshops and industry exposure are "additional value-added activities … not separately charged". The first 50 registrations got 5 Starbucks buy-1-free-1 vouchers. [SITE]
- **Refunds:** "Refund policy will be confirmed by the organising committee." Contact josephine@penangdigitalisation.com. [SITE]
- **Registration form asks for:** name, email, phone; university, course, year; team members' names and emails; preferred workplace problem. [SITE]
- **Problem choice:** you choose at registration. The organisers "confirm the final assignment". You are "not locked in at the point of registering." [SITE]
- **Data consent:** the form asks you to consent to sharing your name, resume and submission with industry partners for "talent and recruitment consideration". The site also says data is used "for programme-related communication and relevant internship or career opportunities, with your explicit consent". [SITE]
- **No guarantee of a job:** "Participation does not guarantee employment." The showcase "is not an offer of employment, investment or funding." [SITE]

## 4. The five challenges

Pick ONE. Full text of each is in `reference/AIFM_2026_Student_Challenge_Guide.md`. [PDF]

| # | Name | One-line task | The guide's "IMPORTANT" rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | SCREEN: Beyond the CV | Review and screen many applications faster | "Do not let AI silently reject people. Show evidence, missing information and reasons for review." |
| 2 | MATCH: Better Talent Matching | Match the right candidate to the right role | "Avoid one unexplained match score. Show why the match exists and what still needs human confirmation." |
| 3 | HIRE: Fairer & Faster Hiring | Improve ONE step of hiring after shortlisting | "Final hiring decisions remain accountable to people." |
| 4 | RETAIN: Early Employee Disengagement | Understand employee difficulties earlier | "Do not secretly monitor employees or infer emotions from cameras or a single behaviour. Use transparent, voluntary information." |
| 5 | GROW: Purpose, Potential & Career Growth | Find strengths, gaps and internal growth paths | "Do not claim AI can predict a person's fixed potential or ideal career. Recommendations should remain explainable and discussable." |

**Minimum output for every challenge:** real input → AI process → clear output → human action.

Note: the site's one-liners for the five problems differ slightly from the PDF (for example "Hire Fairer & Faster" on the site adds "inclusive"). The PDF is the detailed brief. [SITE] [PDF]

## 6. Judging criteria (100%)

[PDF p.9] [SITE]. Both sources agree.

| Weight | Criterion | What judges look for |
|---|---|---|
| 15% | Problem Identification & Understanding | Clear, specific HR problem; who is affected; what happens today; why it matters; evidence or root cause. |
| 20% | AI Solution & Functionality | Working and useful solution; clear role for AI; meaningful improvement; important decisions stay understandable and reviewable by people. |
| 20% | Innovation & Creativity | Original or thoughtful idea; creative use of AI; better than an obvious or purely manual solution; strong user experience. |
| 15% | Business & Economic Impact | Time or cost saved, productivity, hiring quality, retention, better decisions; practical value, feasibility, scalability. |
| 10% | ESG Impact | Fairness, privacy, inclusion, accessibility, wellbeing, governance, efficient resource use. "You do not need to cover every ESG area." |
| 20% | Pitch, Live Demo & Teamwork | Clear story in the time limit; convincing live demo; strong Q&A; good teamwork; **every member understands and can explain the project.** |

Extra points from the videos:
- **Solo teams are still scored on the 20% pitch/demo/teamwork criterion.** The organiser said it still counts for 20% if you are alone. [VID 00a ~31:00–32:30, unclear audio]
- **Judges come from industry**, "not from our teachers". They ask about the problem. If you cannot answer, you lose points. [VID 00a ~21:00–24:50]
- Round 1 judges look for "a working prototype and a clear account of the problem it solves." [SITE]
- The Final has two special tracks: Innovation (most original idea) and Teamwork (strongest collaboration). [SITE]
- The scoring method (who scores, how scores combine, tie-breaks) is not published.

## 9. Responsible AI: the rules that run through every document

1. **Humans decide.** AI assists. People review and make people decisions. [PDF p.8, p.9, p.10, challenge boxes]
2. **No silent rejection.** Show evidence, gaps and reasons. [PDF Ch.1]
3. **No unexplained scores.** [PDF Ch.2]
4. **No secret monitoring. No emotion inference from cameras or one behaviour.** Use voluntary, transparent data. [PDF Ch.4]
5. **No claim of predicting fixed potential.** [PDF Ch.5]
6. **Safe sample data only.** [PDF p.11]
7. **Slide 9 of the pitch is "Responsible AI".** Judges expect you to say what humans must review and how you reduce bias, errors, privacy and misuse risk. [PDF p.10]
8. From the Industry Problem workshop (an organiser-side trainer, so close to official thinking): check every AI idea with "improve a real situation without creating a new harm" and "what new risk could AI create?" Ask what data it uses, whether it labels quiet people as a problem, who reviews it, and whether an employee can challenge it. [VID 03 ~31:20–33:46]

## 10. What the workshops taught (condensed)

Treat this as advice from speakers. It is not a rule set. Timestamps point to the transcripts.

### 10.1 Briefing videos (organiser)
- Journey: **case → define the problem → AI → build → demo.** "Don't jump straight in." [00a ~01:58–02:44]
- Separate **fact from assumption**. Define the problem with Who / Problem / Why. Use one or two sentences. [00a ~03:00–04:21, 00b ~05:50–06:30]
- Then ask where AI helps: analyse, find patterns, recommend, automate. Choose a tool to fit the idea. [00a, 00b]
- "Do not panic": you need one prototype. Dashboard, website OR app. [00a ~06:16–08:35]
- "You don't need a perfect solution on day one." Start with one problem, make the idea clear, build, then improve. [00a ~10:17–10:47]
- A **mentor** is arranged for each team after workshops 1–2. [00a ~34:29]
- The organisers expect industry-practical answers. "This is not a school assignment." [00a ~18:25]

### 10.2 Workshop 1: Pitching (Ts Lukas J Tan, PDX organising chairman) [OPINION]
- **Why → Who → What**, then How and Future as supporting material. [~43:49–45:30]
- A 6-step slide outline: who I am; what problem; why I made it; the solution; future vision; the ask. [~46:20–49:55]
- **Use only 70–80% of your time**, so judges can ask questions. "Don't use up the maximum time." [~50:47]
- Max 10 slides. Put more in an appendix. [~50:05]
- Lead with a story and emotion, not specs. Too much text and AI-written wording make judges lose focus. "Golden rule": clean up and remove the unnecessary. [~33:54, 52:11–53:00]
- Dress professionally. People "buy you before your idea". [~53:47–54:08]
- Make a realistic **ask** and say what the judge gains. Dare to ask. [~49:09–49:55]
- With a strong "why" you can present without slides. Slides are a supporting tool. [~56:10]
- The pitching trainer said the exact time limit was unclear in his session. He told students to ask in chat. [~55:19]

### 10.3 Workshop 2: Future-Ready You (Dr Hiro Koo, IO psychologist) [OPINION]
No competition rules. Ideas that map to HR products:
- Make AI tools a "**first small step**" people can adopt. Avoid overwhelming them. [~09:00, 42:25]
- Do not trigger shame. Frame wellbeing tools as development, not a problem. [~21:17–22:34]
- Fix the **right level**: individual vs organisation. Guessing the cause wrongly wastes training money. [~10:05–18:06]
- Gap idea: organisation-level "pulse" tools for psychosocial safety climate are rare. Annual assessments come too late. [~53:02–55:29]
- Strengths can be overused or underused. [~24:14–30:00]
- Consider who will buy it and who benefits. A tool that helps one department can hurt another. [~23:49, 59:34]
- Make it **HR-claimable** if cost stops adoption (HRD Corp, Malaysia). [~42:53–44:10, garbled]

### 10.4 Workshop 3: Industry Problem & AI Discovery (Christine Chua, CKM) [organiser-side trainer]
- Framework: **See → Ask → Challenge → Define → Discover.** Only then ask where AI helps, "including the possibility that AI is not needed." [~20:46–23:34]
- "You are not being given the answer. You are being given a situation and clues." You must "define a problem you can defend". [~29:17]
- Simulated case: 400 applications, 6 working days to screen, 1 in 4 shortlisted candidates rejected by the hiring manager, some candidates withdraw after more than a week. Three readings: screening too slow; criteria unclear between HR and manager; hand-off to interview too slow. These numbers are challenge data, not real employer data. [~24:35–26:50]
- Warning: "we might build an AI screening tool that rejects candidates faster. We have made the original mistake more efficient." [~17:33]
- Five-point check before committing: real problem? understand the affected person? cause or symptom? is AI useful here? what changes if solved well? [~47:31]
- "Fall in love with the problem, not the solution." [~48:43]
- Competition needs: "a working solution, a live demo, a strong [pitch?], and a clear explanation of its value and impact." [~48:19]
- Your first idea will change after you talk to people. "That is a sign of good discovery." [~48:06]
- A guide per problem was promised to all students after the session. Check your email for it. [~50:32]

### 10.5 Workshop 4: AI Solution & Creativity Lab (Ricky Soo, AICoach.my) [OPINION]
Design-thinking pipeline, one tool per step. Worked example: staff hand-over knowledge loss.

| Step | Tool shown | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| Empathise | NotebookLM | Upload interview transcripts. Ask questions with citations. |
| Define | FigJam AI | Cluster responses into themes. Draft a "How Might We" statement. |
| Ideate | Miro AI | Generate idea categories. |
| Prototype | **Google AI Studio** ("vibe coding") | Build a working app from a prompt. First build about 10 minutes. Ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude to write a PRD, then paste it in. |
| Test | **Maze** | Run a user test with a shared link. Maze AI writes the report. |

Other points:
- One chatbot can do all of it if you prefer. Different tools were shown to compare.
- The prototype can be non-technical. The idea can be a programme or training, not necessarily software. [~36:31, 50:14]
- A good sign in the demo app: it refused to answer a salary question and said to ask the manager.
- "You can't say 'AI did it, I don't know'. That isn't a student skill." This matches the guide's rule that every teammate must be able to explain the project. [~64:10]
- Test with real people, not only yourself. [~49:55]
